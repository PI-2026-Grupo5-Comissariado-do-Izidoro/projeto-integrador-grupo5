The Battle

Dust from the terrain still hung in the air when the battle ended. The clamor of shouting soldiers gradually faded, giving way to an eerie silence. Near a ruined wall, an enemy soldier lay on the ground—wounded and unable to move. He was unarmed and posed no further threat. An officer approached and ordered a young soldier to kill the man. The soldier took a few steps forward and leveled his rifle at the fallen figure; yet, upon looking at him, he saw no monster or active combatant, but rather a terrified young man in a tattered uniform, gripped by fear. The officer repeated the order, this time with greater sternness. The young soldier stood frozen, unable to pull the trigger. He knew that disobeying a superior could lead to serious trouble, but he also knew that killing someone who had already surrendered would be an act of profound cowardice. In that moment, he had to choose between following an order and doing what was morally right. "He has already surrendered and is wounded. He can no longer fight," the soldier said, lowering his weapon. The officer was angered by the young man’s stance, but another soldier intervened, calling for a medical team to tend to the prisoner. The man was subsequently taken away to receive care and placed under military custody. Years later, the soldier could barely recall the medals he had earned or the tactical strategies he had learned during the war. Yet, he never forgot that day. He came to realize that human rights and citizenship do not exist merely in books or laws; they manifest in our own actions. For respecting the dignity of another person remains vital—even in the most difficult circumstances, and even when everyone around us seems to think otherwise.
